Time in UniNet begins in the simplest possible way: as a count.
The index $n$ is not a coordinate and not something an observer measures directly. It is just the order in which updates happen — a shared rhythm that says this comes after that. Each tick marks one application of the local update rule across the graph, nothing more and nothing less.
What matters is not the size of a tick, but the fact that ticks are discrete and ordered. Because updates are local, each tick allows information to move at most one edge away. From that alone, delays appear. Reachability becomes constrained. Causal order emerges. Long before spacetime is introduced, the graph already knows what can influence what, and when.
Importantly, the tick itself carries no direction beyond ordering. The underlying update is reversible, and nothing about $n$ prefers a past or a future. The arrow of time only appears later, when states are viewed through boundaries and coarse‑grained projections. At the level of the tick, time is simply the bookkeeper’s clock: a way to say that updates happen one after another, and that influence takes time to travel.
Seen this way, $n$ is not yet physical time — it is the scaffolding from which physical time can later be built. Geometry, delay, and eventually observer‑time are all layered on top of this simple ordering. The tick keeps the model honest, without asking it to commit too early to any particular notion of duration or flow.
