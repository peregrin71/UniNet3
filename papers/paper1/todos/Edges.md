Edges — a friendly way to think about them
If nodes are where information can pause, then edges are where information is allowed to move.
An edge is not a wire and it is not a force. It is simply a declared adjacency: a statement that information at one node may influence another, but only by passing through that connection. Because updates are local, edges are the only routes information can take. Nothing jumps. Nothing shortcuts. Every influence traces a path edge by edge.
Edges are where delay enters the system. Traversing an edge takes time — at least one update step — and that simple fact is enough to impose a causal order on the graph. Light‑cone–like structures, horizons, and Regge‑style discrete geometry all follow from this alone. What happens inside a node may vary, but edges fix when influence can arrive elsewhere.
Seen operationally, edges are also boundaries in miniature. They limit throughput, constrain flux, and define what can cross from one local interior to another. Congestion does not happen on edges because they “fill up,” but because too much interior content is trying to pass through too few connections. In that sense, edges quietly set the stage for bottlenecks, delays, and eventually the need for buffering.
Edges do not store information. They mediate it. They are the grammar of adjacency — the rules that say who can talk to whom, and how fast. Everything else, from causality to geometry, is written on top of that simple constraint.
