### Kaons as Two‑Anchor Boundary Modes with Chiral Interference

Neutral kaons provide the simplest setting in which the boundary dynamics of a
two‑anchor system interact with a fixed chiral asymmetry.  
Unlike baryons, whose three‑anchor geometry rigidly constrains their boundary
harmonics, mesons are defined by only two anchors on the global spherical
boundary. This under‑constrained geometry allows their phase pattern to drift,
rotate, and interfere with its own mirror configuration.

A neutral kaon is therefore not a localized object but a *boundary harmonic*
defined by a quark–antiquark anchor pair. Two such harmonics are possible:



\[
K^0 : (d,\;\bar{s}), \qquad 
\bar{K}^0 : (\bar{d},\;s).
\]



These are two distinct orientations of the same two‑anchor pattern on the same
global boundary. Because they share the same surface, the two patterns can
superpose and interfere. The resulting boundary state is a time‑dependent
combination of the two modes,



\[
|\Psi(t)\rangle = a(t)\,|K^0\rangle + b(t)\,|\bar{K}^0\rangle,
\]



but the essential physics is geometric rather than algebraic:  
the two phase‑gradient patterns drift across the sphere and continually move
into and out of alignment.

#### Fixed chiral tilt

The update operator on the boundary contains a small, fixed chiral asymmetry.
Geometrically, this acts like a tiny global “tilt” or directional bias on the
sphere. It does not fluctuate in time and does not depend on the kaon pattern.
It is a structural property of the boundary dynamics.

This fixed chiral tilt interacts with the drifting kaon pattern.  
Because the kaon’s phase gradient is free to rotate and precess, the *effective*
coupling between the kaon mode and the chiral tilt is time‑dependent even though
the tilt itself is fixed. At some moments the phase gradient aligns with the
tilt; at others it misaligns or even opposes it.

#### Interference and time‑dependent coupling

The two kaon modes form a pair of independent two‑anchor harmonics. Their
interference produces a slowly varying phase pattern on the boundary. As this
pattern drifts relative to the fixed chiral tilt, the effective coupling
strength oscillates. When the phase gradient aligns with the tilt, the boundary
can propagate the pattern slightly more easily; when misaligned, propagation is
slightly hindered.

This alignment–misalignment cycle is the geometric origin of kaon oscillations.
The familiar two‑state mixing is simply the interference of two drifting
boundary modes under a fixed directional bias.

#### Congestion and decay

Because the kaon pattern is under‑constrained, there are moments when the
interference between the two modes, combined with the fixed chiral tilt,
produces a locally steep phase gradient on the boundary. At such moments the
update operator cannot propagate the pattern smoothly. Local congestion builds
up.

In the boundary framework, **congestion is the trigger for decay**.  
When the local gradient exceeds what the boundary can sustain, the system
relaxes into a simpler harmonic—typically a multi‑pion pattern. The short‑lived
and long‑lived kaon modes correspond to patterns that encounter congestion
frequently or rarely, respectively.

The rare CP‑violating decay channels arise when the drifting phase gradient
momentarily aligns with the chiral tilt in a way that produces congestion in a
region that would otherwise remain stable. The small asymmetry in these
alignment conditions is the geometric origin of the observed CP violation.

#### Summary

Neutral kaons are two‑anchor boundary harmonics on a single global sphere.  
Their two possible orientations interfere, drift, and periodically align or
misalign with a fixed chiral tilt in the update operator. When this alignment
produces a locally unsustainable phase gradient, congestion builds up and the
kaon decays. CP violation emerges naturally as a small asymmetry in the
alignment conditions of the drifting pattern with the fixed chiral bias.
