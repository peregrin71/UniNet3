# Leakiness as Boundary‑Visible Delay  
### A companion to the *Gaussian Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics*

This note extends the interior–boundary perspective developed in the *Gaussian Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics*. In that paper, the central idea is that an observer never interacts with the interior of a system directly. All accessible information arrives through the boundary, and the boundary is always a compressed outward-facing summary of whatever interior structure produced it. Many different interiors can lead to the same boundary signature, and the observer’s task is to infer what they can from that limited outward pattern.

Leakiness fits naturally into this picture. It refers to the way interior structure becomes visible at the boundary through timing, phase shifts, or oscillation patterns. In the Gaussian paper, this appears in a static form: the boundary signature is what the observer receives, and the interior is inferred from that signature. Leakiness adds a small dynamical layer to the same idea. The time it takes for information to reach the boundary can also carry traces of the interior, without ever revealing the interior directly.

This does not introduce new degrees of freedom. It simply highlights that delay is another way the interior leaves a faint imprint on the boundary. The interior remains inaccessible; the boundary remains the only interface. Leakiness is just the operational shadow of that asymmetry.

The sections below collect examples from different areas of physics where experiments already observe this kind of boundary‑visible delay. Each field has its own vocabulary for it, so the unity is easy to overlook. The aim is to show how a boundary‑first perspective can make these patterns feel more connected, and how the same interior–boundary logic appears across very different physical systems.

---

## 1. Particle physics  
Experiments often measure arrival times, oscillation phases, or decay patterns. These are exactly the kinds of boundary‑visible effects that leakiness describes.

### Neutrino oscillations  
Neutrinos change flavor depending on how far they travel and what they pass through. Detectors only see when they arrive and which flavor they show up as. In a boundary‑first view, the interior of the medium leaves a timing imprint on the boundary. The oscillation pattern is a delay‑weighted shadow of the interior refractive structure.

### Meson mixing  
Systems like \(K^0\)–\(\bar{K}^0\) or \(B^0\)–\(\bar{B}^0\) oscillate between states with characteristic lifetimes. The boundary sees only the time distribution of decays, but that timing reflects the internal mode structure of the composite system. Interior → delay → boundary.

### Proton structure functions  
Deep inelastic scattering shows that the proton “looks different” depending on how you probe it. In a leakiness picture, different probes couple to different delay‑weighted paths through the proton’s interior. The boundary sees timing and phase; the interior geometry shapes them.

---

## 2. Nuclear physics  
Timing differences are often treated as puzzles, but they fit naturally into a leakiness picture.

### Neutron lifetime discrepancy  
Bottle and beam experiments infer different neutron lifetimes because they impose different effective boundaries. Different boundaries lead to different leakiness patterns. The interior is the same; the boundary conditions are not.

### Medium‑dependent decay rates  
Nuclear environments sometimes shift decay rates. This can be understood as interior congestion or path‑length changes affecting the delay before information reaches the boundary.

---

## 3. Condensed matter  
These systems are almost literal playgrounds for boundary‑first thinking.

### Topological insulators  
Edge modes propagate along the boundary, but their timing and stability depend on the bulk topology. The boundary sees only arrival patterns, yet those patterns encode the interior’s structure. This is leakiness in a clean and direct form.

### Josephson junctions  
Phase delays across a junction reflect the coherence structure inside the superconductors. The boundary sees a phase shift; the interior determines the delay.

### Quantum wells and dots  
Emission delays and tunneling times reveal the shape and connectivity of the confining region. The boundary sees timing; the interior leaves its shadow through delay.

---

## 4. High‑energy and QCD systems  
Here the interior is extremely complex, but the boundary still only sees timing and phase.

### Hadronization times  
In heavy‑ion collisions, the time it takes for quark–gluon plasma to re‑form hadrons reflects interior connectivity and congestion. The boundary sees formation times; the interior determines them.

### Proton radius puzzle  
Different probes infer different radii because they interact with different effective paths inside the proton. Each path has its own delay signature. The proton is the same; the boundary coupling differs.

---

## 5. Cosmology and astrophysics  
On cosmic scales, leakiness becomes visible as timing differences across enormous distances.

### CMB acoustic peaks  
The timing of acoustic oscillations in the early universe encodes the interior structure of the primordial plasma. The boundary (our telescopes) sees phase‑shifted peaks — a timing shadow of the early interior.

### Gravitational wave dispersion  
Propagation delays through curved or structured spacetime reveal interior geometry. The boundary sees arrival times; the interior shapes them.

### Lensing time delays  
Multiple light paths around a massive object arrive at different times. The boundary sees delay; the interior mass distribution creates it.

---

## 6. Shared structure across fields  
Across all these examples, the same pattern appears:

- The interior is rich and often inaccessible.  
- The boundary sees only timing, phase, or oscillation patterns.  
- These patterns are leakiness: the way interior structure becomes visible through delay.  
- Different fields give different names to the same effect.

A boundary‑first perspective makes this unity easier to see. The Gaussian Interpretation describes the static version of this asymmetry; leakiness is the dynamical version. Both express the same idea: the interior is inferred, never inspected, and the boundary is the only place where information becomes available.

---

## 7. Closing remarks  
Leakiness is not a new phenomenon. It is already present in many experiments, from neutrino oscillations to Josephson junctions to gravitational waves. What changes in a boundary‑first view is the recognition that these effects share a common structure. Delay is not an extra degree of freedom; it is simply another way the interior leaves a faint imprint on the boundary. This perspective connects naturally to the Gaussian Interpretation and extends it into the dynamical domain, where timing and phase carry the same interior–boundary logic that amplitudes and collapse express in the static domain.
